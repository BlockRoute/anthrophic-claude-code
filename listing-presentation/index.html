Property

Inspections

In Virginia, most buyers write a home inspection contingency into their offer — typically a window of about 7–10 days after ratification to have the property professionally inspected. Based on the report, the buyer can accept the home as-is, request repairs or credits, or in many cases walk away. Inspection surprises are one of the most common reasons a contract falls apart, so we prepare for the inspection before we ever list.

PREPARING TO HIT THE MARKET
Home

Staging

After pricing, presentation is the most powerful lever you control. Before your home is photographed, we’ll walk it together room by room and build a focused prep list — what to declutter, what to touch up, and what to leave alone. The goal isn’t a magazine shoot; it’s helping buyers instantly picture themselves living there.

  • Curb AppealFresh mulch, trimmed shrubs, and a mowed lawn are the baseline. Small touches — pressure washing, a re-stained front door, polished hardware, a working doorbell — set the tone before buyers ever step inside.
  • Kitchens & BathroomsThese rooms carry the sale. Clear the counters, make appliances and glass shine, and tuck away everyday items like toothbrushes and cleaning products for photos and showings.
  • FlooringWorn carpet is one of the most common buyer objections. Replace it if it’s tired, deep-clean it if it’s salvageable, and refresh tile grout — floors quietly signal how well the whole home was kept.
  • Walls & TrimFresh, neutral paint is consistently the highest-return dollar you can spend preparing a home. Crisp walls and trim read as “move-in ready” — and buyers pay for move-in ready.

Quick Wins Before Every Showing

•  Remove entry, kitchen, and bath mats to open up floor space

•  Clean windows inside and out

•  Match all light bulbs and replace any that are out

•  Keep scents subtle — strong fragrance reads as a cover-up

•  Stow pet bowls, beds, and litter boxes out of sight

•  When in doubt, less: fewer items make rooms feel larger

MARKETING STRATEGY
Open Houses

Fact vs. Fiction

Open houses can absolutely play a role in selling your home — but they work best as one tool among many, not as the whole strategy. Before we decide whether and when to hold one, here’s an honest look at the common myths:

Myth: Only serious buyers walk through an open house

Reality: Many open-house visitors haven’t spoken with a lender and don’t yet know what they can afford. Buyers touring by private appointment with an agent are far more likely to be pre-qualified and genuinely in the market.

Myth: The neighbors won’t bother coming

Reality: They will — and that’s not all bad. Curious neighbors compare your home to theirs, and they often know the exact friend or family member who’s been wanting to move into the neighborhood.

Myth: You must hold an open house to sell

Reality: Homes sell every week without one. An agent who leans entirely on open houses is often substituting them for the online marketing that actually reaches today’s buyers.

Myth: Open houses never sell homes

Reality: The odds of a direct sale are modest, but open houses build traffic, buzz, and urgency — and occasionally produce the buyer. When it serves your sale, I’ll host it and work it properly.

A word of strategy: too many open houses can make a listing look anxious. Spacing them out — and pairing one with any price improvement — keeps each event working in your favor.

MARKETING STRATEGY
Smart Homes &

Recording Buyers

Doorbell cameras, indoor cameras, and smart speakers are everywhere — but during showings they can create real legal risk. Before your home hits the market, let’s make sure your technology doesn’t become a liability.

  • Audio Is the Danger ZoneVirginia’s consent rules for recording conversations generally protect you only when you are a party to the conversation. Capturing audio of buyers talking privately inside your home — a conversation you’re not part of — can expose you to serious liability under state and federal law. When in doubt, turn audio recording off during showings.
  • Video Is Safer, With LimitsSilent video from cameras covering common areas — the exterior, entry, or garage — is generally lower risk. Cameras should never cover private spaces like bathrooms, and combining video with audio during a showing is never worth the exposure.
  • Visitors Have Privacy RightsAn invitation into your home is not consent to be recorded however you like. Privacy law protects the people walking through your door, and courts take overreach seriously. If you have questions about your specific setup, consult an attorney before listing.
  • The Better Way to Hear BuyersEavesdropping isn’t just risky — it’s unnecessary. Honest, structured feedback comes through your agent, and it’s far more useful than anything overheard on a doorbell camera.

Before We List

Tell me about every recording device in the home. We’ll decide together what gets disabled, what gets disclosed, and how to keep your sale — and you — protected.

The

Appraisal

  • What HappensOnce inspection matters are resolved, the buyer’s lender orders an appraisal. The appraiser visits the home — usually for well under an hour — to measure, photograph, and evaluate it against recent comparable sales. I coordinate access and timing so it’s painless for you.
  • Why It MattersThe appraisal protects the lender, but it effectively sets a ceiling on the financed price. If the appraised value comes in at or above the contract price, we proceed as planned. If it comes in short, the price or terms get renegotiated — and I’ll be armed with the comparable sales data to fight for your number.
  • How We PrepareBefore the appraiser arrives, I provide a package of the strongest recent comparables, along with a list of your home’s upgrades and improvements. Appraisers are independent, but complete information leads to accurate values.
  • The ResultsResults typically arrive within about a week. I’ll review them with you the day they land and explain exactly what they mean for your closing.

A Pricing Reality Check

The appraisal is one more reason strategic pricing wins: a contract price the market can’t support often gets pulled back to earth at the appraisal anyway. Pricing right from the start means the appraisal confirms your value instead of threatening your deal.

  • “Clear to Close”When the buyer’s lender issues a clear to close, every financing condition has been satisfied — the loan is fully approved. From here, the settlement agent prepares the final numbers.

The Final

Walk-Through

Shortly before closing, the buyers will walk the home one last time. Their goal is simple: confirm the property is in the condition they agreed to buy, that negotiated repairs are complete, and that the move-out didn’t leave damage behind. The golden rule: leave the home the way you’d hope to find it if you were the one moving in.

Your walk-through checklist
  • Patch and touch up nail holes as you take down décor — start early so you’re not rushing during the move. A small artist’s brush and matching paint make the work disappear.
  • Remove all trash and personal items, and have the home cleaned after the movers finish.
  • Have the lawn cut within a few days of closing so the exterior looks as good as the interior.
  • Leave manuals, warranties, garage remotes, and mailbox keys together in a kitchen drawer; bring spare house keys to closing.
  • Complete any negotiated repairs before the walk-through and keep the receipts to share.
  • Plan to be fully moved out by the evening before closing, so the buyers can schedule their walk-through without pressure.

If Something Goes Wrong

If anything is damaged during the move-out, tell me immediately — a repair, a credit, or a simple conversation almost always solves it. What causes closing delays isn’t the problem; it’s the surprise.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS
After the Offer

Seller FAQ

Should I be home for the buyer’s inspection?

No — this is the buyer’s time with the home and their inspector. They’ll speak more freely, and the process moves faster, when the owner isn’t present.

Do I have to agree to the buyer’s repair requests?

No. Repair requests — and requests for credits in place of repairs — are negotiable like everything else in the contract. We’ll respond with a strategy, not a reflex.

Can we keep showing the house after accepting an offer?

You can. Some sellers continue showings for backup offers, though you can’t accept another offer unless the current contract falls through. In practice, most listings move to pending status and showings taper off — we’ll pick the approach that fits your situation.

When should we start packing?

Seriously packing? Once repairs are negotiated and the appraisal has come back — that’s when the transaction’s biggest hurdles are behind you.

Is the closing date guaranteed?

Treat it as a target, not a promise. Financed purchases commonly take 30–45+ days for full loan approval, and the precise date and time are usually confirmed only in the final days before closing. I’ll keep you posted at every step.

What happens if the buyer’s financing falls through?

We assess quickly and relist or move to a backup offer with minimal lost time. It’s also why we weigh financing strength — not just price — when choosing an offer in the first place.
